Privacy policy

Desktag collects no data. It has no account, no analytics and no ads, and its App Sandbox gives it no way to reach the network. What it keeps stays in one file on your Mac.

Last updated 22 November 2026. This policy covers the Desktag app for Mac and this website.

What Desktag collects

Nothing. The developer of Desktag receives no information from the app: not your desktops’ names, not your notes, not which apps you use, and not how you use Desktag.

This is enforced by macOS, not only promised. Desktag runs in the App Sandbox with no network permission, so the app cannot open a connection to any server. It contains no analytics, advertising or crash-reporting code, and it does not track you.

What Desktag keeps on your Mac

Desktag saves what it needs in one file inside its own sandbox folder:

~/Library/Containers/dev.deskmate.desktag/Data/Library/Application Support/Desktag/desktops.json

The file holds:

  • the emoji, name, colour, marks, note and keyboard shortcut you give each desktop;
  • your settings, such as the sticker’s layout and where you dragged it;
  • when Desktag first met each desktop and when you last visited it;
  • for each desktop, which apps had windows on it, each window’s number, position and size, and a code that tells one login session from the next. Desktag uses this to recognise the desktop after a restart. It never records a window’s title or anything inside a window;
  • for each app whose windows you have dragged, where on a window you held it (a distance from one side and from the top) and how big that window was, so Desktag can hold that app’s windows there when moving one for you;
  • if you use Spatial Canvas or No-Boundary Canvas, how you arranged it: where each desktop bubble sits, and the name, colour and desktops of each group you made;
  • how many times you have switched desktops with Desktag, used only to decide when to ask, at most once per version, whether you would like to rate it.

Beside it, Desktag keeps a backup of the previous version of that file (desktops.json.bak) and, in a Backups folder, a copy for each of the last seven days on which something changed. It also keeps a local journal of what it decided (journal.log, the last thousand lines), such as “walk: 9 desktops, 2 by position” or “moved a window”: counts and kinds only, never a name, a note, a title or an app. In its preferences it remembers what the App Store last said about your purchase, the latest date it has seen (so setting the Mac’s clock back doesn’t lengthen the trial), and two facts about how your Mac switches desktops.

Desktag sends none of this anywhere. Settings › About › Copy Diagnostics puts a short report on your clipboard for you to send to support if you choose: counts, settings and the journal, never a name, a note or an app. Like the rest of your Library folder, these files are included in backups you make of your Mac, such as Time Machine. To delete them, see Uninstalling Desktag.

What Desktag can see

To tell desktops apart, Desktag asks macOS for the list of windows. Without any permission, macOS reports which app owns each window and where the window is.

To name an unnamed desktop after the folders open on it, and to show agents at work, Desktag also reads what macOS tells any app about your own processes: which programs run in each of your terminals, which app started them, which folder they’re in, what command started them (its arguments, never its environment) and when, and how busy they keep your Mac. Where Desktag shows a command, passwords, tokens and keys in it are masked. To tell which window a terminal is in, it lines up when the terminal’s shells started with the order its windows opened in, and asks macOS how long ago a key was pressed, never which key. It never reads what a terminal displays, the keys you press or anything a program writes, and keeps none of this on disk: it is worked out again from what is running, and forgotten when Desktag quits.

Screen Recording is optional, and Desktag asks for it only when you choose to allow it: Allow… in Settings, Allow Window Titles… in a desktop’s card, or Show Window Titles… in the sticker’s menu. With it, macOS gives Desktag each window’s title (for Finder’s folders, editors’ projects, pages and documents, and what an agent says it’s working on) and lets Desktag take a momentary in-memory frame of the desktop to blend smoothly during custom switch transitions or hold the screen steady while silently syncing desktops after a restart. Neither window titles nor momentary frames are ever recorded, written to disk, or kept once the moment passes. While “Hide panels from screen sharing” is on in Settings › General, Desktag asks macOS to leave its panels out of screen sharing and screenshots. Most apps honour that request, though some recording tools capture every window regardless, and a mirrored display shows everything: for those there is Private Mode, below.

Without any permission, Desktag also notices when a window in front moves along with the pointer while the mouse button is down: that is how it knows you are dragging a window over the sticker, and where you are holding it. It learns nothing else from your mouse.

The Accessibility permission is optional. Desktag asks for it only when you choose something that needs it: Find Desktops, instant switching, sending a window to another desktop, or enabling it in Settings. With it, Desktag posts input as you would make it: trackpad desktop swipes, ⌃← and ⌃→, a press, drag and release on a window’s title bar to carry it to another desktop, and a click on a window’s title bar to focus it from a card. It does not use the permission to read your screen, keystrokes or other apps; macOS’s sandbox prevents reading other apps in any case.

Private Mode

Private Mode, in the menu bar menu, in Settings, and through macOS Focus Filters, keeps window titles, folders, notes and agent tasks out of everything Desktag shows: the sticker, cards, the switcher, banners and notifications. Turning it on also clears any Desktag notifications waiting in Notification Centre. The names you gave your desktops stay, and unnamed ones show open apps. It turns on automatically while your screen is mirrored and off when mirroring ends, unless you turn that off in Settings.

Notifications

Notifications are off until you turn them on in Settings › General; macOS then asks you once whether Desktag may post them. When work stops on a desktop you aren’t on, Desktag asks macOS to show a notification on this Mac naming the agent or command, the desktop and what it worked on (in Private Mode, not what it worked on). It stays in Notification Centre until you go to that desktop. Notifications are posted locally by macOS on your Mac and go nowhere else.

Purchases

Desktag Pro and the free 14-day trial are obtained through the Mac App Store. Apple handles payment under Apple’s privacy policy. Desktag learns only whether the Apple Account signed in on your Mac owns Pro or has started the trial, and when. It never receives your name, email address or payment details.

Apple provides developers aggregated sales totals by country and, from users who opt in to share diagnostics with app developers, anonymised crash reports and usage figures. None of it identifies you.

Shortcuts, Spotlight, Focus Filters, Services, scripting & links

Desktag’s 13 actions in the Shortcuts app, its local CoreSpotlight index for named desktops, and its AppleScript / JXA dictionary expose only the desktop names, emoji, numbers and marks you gave them on this Mac — never auto-read window titles, and never notes in Private Mode. What a shortcut or script does with a name is up to your workflow. Move Front Window to Desktop and Find Desktops post input as Desktag does when you invoke them, and need Accessibility access. macOS Focus Filters let a Focus toggle Private Mode, hide the sticker or silence banners automatically. macOS Services send selected text from another app to name the active desktop or append to its note only when you invoke them. desktag:// links only move between desktops (without clicking into windows) or open the switcher; a link asking Desktag to find desktops only shows a confirmation button for you to start it. Links cannot change a label or setting.

This website

These pages set no cookies, use no web storage, run no analytics or tracking, and load no fonts, scripts or images from third-party servers. Interactive previews on the home page and topic filtering on the support page run locally in your browser without transmitting any data. The static web host that serves these pages (GitHub Pages) receives standard HTTP request logs including your IP address and requested page path, as every web server does.
